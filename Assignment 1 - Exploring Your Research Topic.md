# Assignment 1 — Exploring Your Research Topic

**Week 1 · one PDF file**

This is a starting point, not a research proposal. You are asked to look at two or three
topics you might study, compare them honestly, pick one provisionally, and write down what
you still do not know. That is the whole task.

You do **not** need to prove that a research gap exists, run an experiment, choose a final
method, write polished prose, or produce a finished plan. Those belong to later weeks.

## How this is marked

There are five homeworks in this course. Each one is graded Pass/Fail, and the grade depends
only on the coherence of its content. A Pass is 4 points and a Fail is 0 points. There is no
partial credit, so the five homeworks together are worth 20 points.

**No late submissions will be accepted.**

Coherence means the parts of your submission fit together. The candidate descriptions, the
comparison across the five criteria, the topic you pick provisionally with your reasons, and
the unknowns and next check you write down should all tell the same story. A reader should be
able to see why each candidate is described the way it is, why the same five criteria are
applied to every candidate, why your provisional pick follows from that comparison, and why
the things you still do not know are exactly the things you say you would check next. If one
part contradicts another, the content is not coherent yet.

You are not marked on novelty, on choosing the "correct" topic, on polished English, or on
having a confirmed gap. Any exploratory choice can pass, including "I am still unsure between
two topics", as long as it stays consistent with the rest of what you write.

- Provisional choices are expected. You may change your topic later; write down why.
- If you do not know something, write `unknown` and what you would check. That is acceptable
  when you explain what you would check, and it is consistent with the rest of your submission.
- Nothing here is graded on length, formatting polish, or how sophisticated the topic sounds.

## What you are not being asked to do

- No `RESEARCH-PLAN.md` file. You do not have to create or fill one for this assignment, and
  you should not add one to your submission. One plan file is for real project work later.
- No methods or hypotheses to finalise, no experiments to design or run, no budget to estimate.
- No literature review and no confirmed gap.
- No mandatory setup screenshot or busywork. If your environment had a problem, one optional
  sentence about the blocker is enough. Do not include API keys or full chat logs.

## The five criteria

Compare every candidate on the same five criteria. For each criterion, write one or two
sentences — what you actually know, or `unknown` plus what you would check. No numeric score
is required.

**1. Importance — why would this be worth studying?**
Write who is affected and what decision or problem it touches. "It is interesting to me" is a
legitimate part of the answer; "nobody has studied it" is not, because you have not checked yet.

**2. Disciplinary fit — how does it connect to your degree programme (e.g., Business
Informatics) and this course?**
Write the link to information systems, data, decisions, or organisations. A topic from another
field is fine if you can state the connection in one sentence.

**3. Data access — what data or materials could you realistically get?**
Write the specific material (interviews with a role you can reach, exported reports, public
records, a process you can observe), not "data from a company". Access you have not confirmed
must be labelled `access not confirmed`.

**4. Feasibility — could you manage it with your time, skills, and resources?**
Write what the smallest realistic version looks like and what skill it needs. This course does
not fix your thesis timetable, so do not assume you must finish a full thesis in six weeks.

**5. Answerability — can it become one focused question with observable evidence?**
Write a one-sentence question and the evidence that would speak to it. Do not assume proof,
causality, or that the phenomenon has been observed yet.

## One worked example (hypothetical)

Illustration only — not a model answer, not a finding, not a literature claim. The organisation
in it is invented and its access is not confirmed.

**Topic:** Why staff keep using spreadsheets after a BI dashboard is introduced.

| Criterion | What could be written |
|---|---|
| Importance | Reporting time and version confusion affect daily decisions; an unused dashboard is hard to justify to whoever paid for it. |
| Disciplinary fit | Business Informatics: information systems use, work practices, decision support. |
| Data access | Ideally 3–5 interviews with analysts plus a few anonymised report files in one organisation. `Access not confirmed`; a fallback would be staff I can already reach. |
| Feasibility | Small interview study plus document walkthrough; needs an interview guide and basic thematic coding, no software build. Time: `unknown`, but bounded. |
| Answerability | "In one accessible organisation, how do analysts describe their use of spreadsheets alongside a BI dashboard, if such use is observed?" Evidence: interview accounts and the reports themselves. |

**Provisional question:** In one accessible organisation, how do analysts describe their use of
spreadsheets alongside a BI dashboard, if such use is observed?

Why spreadsheets might persist is a tentative explanation here, not a cause. Possible reasons
(habit, trust in the numbers, report turnaround) are listed only as things an interview could
explore, and none of them has been checked.

## Two more topic sketches (hypothetical, brief)

Short is fine. These show the level of detail expected, not topics you must choose.

  Interviews or a small survey would both fit; `not decided`. Access: managers I can reach.
- **Where do errors enter a small reporting process?** Follow one recurring report from source

## Optional: using AI to explore

These prompts are aids, not a required step, and no paid AI account is needed. You can produce
a perfectly good submission with a notebook and no AI at all. You own the choice of topic; a
model's suggestion is a draft you accept, modify, or reject.

**Prompt 1 — widen the field.** Replace the text in angle brackets with your own information, then paste the prompt. Write `unknown` for details you do not know yet.

> I am a student in <your programme, e.g. Business Informatics> taking a course on research
> planning. My interests are <your interest, in a few words>. I can realistically reach
> <data or people you can access>. I have <hours per week> and these skills: <skills>.
> If any of this is missing, ask me up to three short questions first.
> Then suggest three candidate research topics and put them in one table with five criteria:
> importance, disciplinary fit, data access, feasibility, answerability. Keep every cell to one
> short line. Mark anything you are assuming. Do not invent citations, do not claim a research
> gap, and do not assert that I can get data I have not confirmed. If you have not searched,
> say so; a missing search result is not proof of a gap.

**Prompt 2 — compare and narrow.** Use after you have candidates.

> Here are my candidates: <list them, or paste your table>. Compare them on the same five
> criteria and tell me which one is most realistic for a first short study, naming the single
> most uncertain assumption behind your choice. Then give me the cheapest next piece of
> information I could get to reduce that uncertainty — a person to ask, a page to read, a
> document to request. Not an experiment, and not a reading list.

**Prompt 3 — critique only, optional.** Use if you want a check on relevance, not new ideas.

> Read my provisional choice: <one paragraph>. Point out where it drifts from my stated
> interest, where "importance" is assumed rather than argued, and where data access is
> unrealistic. Do not propose a new topic and do not add citations. Ask me one question if
> something is ambiguous.

If an answer looks confident about a source, treat it as unchecked until you have opened the
source yourself.

## What to submit

One PDF file, named `assignment-1-<name>.pdf` (initials are fine; your real identity is not
required in a public repository). You may write it in Word, Google Docs, Markdown, or any other
tool and export or print it to PDF. Copy the four sections below and fill them in. Keep it short.

**A. Candidate comparison.** The table from above, for two or three candidates, with the five
criteria. Unknowns allowed.

**B. Provisional pick.** Which candidate, and the reason in two or three sentences. Note what
would make you change your mind.

**C. Unknowns and next check.** What you do not know yet (question wording, data access,
whether the problem is real) and the cheapest thing you would do next to find out. This is not
a research plan: no methods section, no timeline, no budget.

**D. AI use, only if you used AI.** Two or three sentences: what you asked, what you adopted,
modified, or rejected, and why. If you did not use AI, write "no AI used" — that is complete.

**Optional line.** If your setup or access had a blocker, one sentence here. No logs, no keys,
no screenshots required.
